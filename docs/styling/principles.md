# Practical diagnostic principles

This is an original synthesis, not a consensus rulebook. Source IDs refer to [sources.md](sources.md).

## Coherence without conformity

Read the outfit as relationships, not a list of good pieces. Ask what the eye notices first, whether the other parts support that focus, and what mood the combination communicates. Similar colors can conceal incompatible surface, construction, or scale. Different colors and references can be joined by shape, material, repetition, or purposeful opposition. Do not require the wearer to copy a complete historical uniform. [D8, D10, S1, S4, S13–S15]

For a claim of incoherence, identify the mechanism: for example, a sharply structured upper half paired with a soft collapsing lower half, or several unrelated focal points competing without a clear hierarchy. Then ask whether that contrast appears intentional and effective in the chosen context. Do not demand a tidy verbal explanation for every successful visual surprise, or treat an unfamiliar aesthetic as failed merely because it is unfamiliar. “Different genres” alone is not a sufficient diagnosis. Even intentional dressing can be unsuccessful; intent changes the target, not the need to judge execution.

A restrained classic outfit, expressive high-low outfit, and practical everyday outfit can all succeed on different terms. Explain whether an alternative makes the result quieter, sharper, softer, more rugged, or more dramatic rather than calling every change an upgrade. Simon Crompton's stated preference for subtle dressing is one preference; André Larnyoh and Ethan Wong make space for more personal combinations. [S5, S14, S15]

Coherence does not require equal visual mass: Aaron Levine's contribution to the taste series welcomes deliberate clashes of genre and volume, including bulky clothes with smaller-looking shoes. George Wang emphasizes contextual judgment; Tony Sylvester emphasizes intention and pleasure. Keep those voices distinct from G. Bruce Boyer's more restrained approach. When an experiment feels confused, constrain one dimension, such as palette, while exploring another; do not mechanically neutralize every surprising element. [F3, F6]

## Fit, cut, silhouette: three separate questions

- **Physical fit:** Does the garment stay in place, permit movement, and avoid unintended strain or collapse? Classic-jacket clues include lapel buckling, collar separation, and pulling when fastened. A raised phone arm can distort all three. Ask about sitting or reaching when relevant.
- **Intended cut:** Is the shoulder extended, body boxy, trouser tapered, leg full, or length cropped by design? A shoulder seam beyond the natural shoulder is not automatically a sizing error. Neither are long sleeves or denim stacking.
- **Ensemble silhouette:** What do the pieces create together? Inspect transitions at shoulders, waist, jacket hem, trouser line, and footwear. Consistently full, consistently narrow, and contrasting volumes can each work. Evaluate controlled shape, not conformity to a single ratio. [D1, D4, D5, S1, S3, S12]

Describe the garment's effect, not a defect in the body. Do not assume that elongating legs, broadening shoulders, or reducing apparent size is everyone's goal. Do not prescribe tapering by default. Distinguish an intentional silhouette from excess cloth that visibly obscures the intended shape, while acknowledging uncertainty about design and comfort. Sizing down a generously designed jacket cannot reliably create a different waist shape; it may only constrict the chest or distort the fastening. Choose an appropriate cut rather than forcing an unsuitable size. Jacket length, fastening position, shoulder treatment, rise, and leg line interact, so assess an alteration against the entire design. [C2, C3]

## Formality and successful high-low

Read attributes together. Smooth, shiny cloth and business-associated construction often pull tailoring toward formality; matte or textured cloth, softer structure, and casual details may make it easier to combine informally. These are tendencies with exceptions, not points on an objective numerical scale. A suit can be casual and a separate jacket can be formal. Pliability, surface irregularity, and weave can distinguish a separate jacket even when colors are close; a distant photograph cannot reliably authenticate those properties. [D3, D6, S2, S6–S8, C7]

For an uncertain mix, look for a connection: compatible drape, shared softness, related visual mass, a stable palette, or a recognizable styling idea. A textured jacket and denim can relate through surface and ease. A more dramatic juxtaposition need not use that same formula. Sneakers with tailoring are not inherently wrong, and adding them alone does not automatically make a business outfit convincingly casual.

If a combination fails, offer the most economical route: move the formal element toward the casual pieces, move the casual pieces toward the formal element, or rebuild around the user's favorite component. Do not always dress everything up. Ethan Wong's role-preserving substitution is useful here: retain the organizing idea while changing what performs a role. [S5, S15]

## Trousers and shoes as a pair

Consider rise and top length together, then follow the seat/thigh, knee, hem, break, and shoe. Read toe shape, shine, sole, and visual bulk, not simply shoe color or category. Look for an abrupt or purposeful transition. Full trousers may pair naturally with substantial shoes, but contrast can be effective too. [D5, S8, S12]

A large cuff, pooling fabric, or narrow opening can be the main issue if it dominates the outline. Otherwise, place hem advice below a larger mismatch. Zero break is not compulsory; stacking can be a coherent denim or streetwear choice. A still image cannot establish whether a hem catches during walking.

Quiet footwear under dark classic tailoring is Derek Guy's contextual convention, not a law for Westernwear or streetwear. Deliberately prominent shoes can be the focal point. [D15]

## Color, texture, and pattern

Start with dominant areas, light-dark contrast, saturation, and focal points. Read the actual shades visible while accounting for white balance. Tonal outfits can be rich through texture, reflectivity, and shape; high contrast can be purposeful. Exact matching is neither required nor forbidden. The size of a colored area matters: a small intense accent and a large block can have very different effects. Surrounding colors, sheen, and viewing distance change the result; do not turn this into skin-tone prohibitions or seasonal typing. [D2, D12, S10, C6]

A subtle echo can connect pieces, but adding an accessory solely to repeat a color may make the result busier. A single leading pattern with supporting pieces is one strategy; multiple patterns of differing scale can also work. Judge their interaction and proximity, not a fixed count. Material can determine coherence and formality, not merely provide a decorative finishing touch. [D8, D14, S1, S11]

## Feasible improvements, not automatic consumption

Change one high-impact variable when that will reveal the cause. Use a tuck, unfastening, removal, reversible cuff, or already-owned alternative when appropriate. Name a needed category without inventing ownership. Recommend buying only when the user wants shopping or an actual wardrobe gap is established. [D7, D9, S9, S15]

Do not promise that a tailor can fix shoulders, jacket length, rise, or insufficient cloth. A temporary test can reveal a visual preference; permanent alteration still needs feasibility and comfort checks. Avoid copying old source prices or treating luxury craftsmanship as a prerequisite.

## Evidence and judgment

Use an evidence ladder: visible observation → plausible interpretation → aesthetic judgment → practical suggestion. Keep these distinct without making every answer labored. For example, “the hem bunches above the shoe” is an observation; “the bottom feels heavier than the jacket” is a judgment. A fabric may look smooth without being identifiable as a specific fiber.

Acknowledge a successful element only when it supports the diagnosis, not to soften every criticism. If the entire grouping lacks a convincing idea, say that clearly and identify the part to retain. Conversely, do not fabricate faults when the ensemble works. Honest disagreement with the user or an admired writer is permitted when supported by evidence.

## Four independent kinds of success

Ask four different questions instead of compressing the assessment into a universal quality ladder:

1. **Practical success:** Does it meet the actual setting, climate, movement, comfort, safety, and dress code?
2. **Expressive success:** What references and mood does it convey? Equally casual outfits can feel soft or rugged, equally tailored outfits quiet or theatrical.
3. **Execution:** Which visible relationships create or undermine that effect?
4. **Preference:** Is this an effect the wearer wants? Do not disguise the critic's taste as a fit defect. [C1, C4]

A visually compelling outfit can fail a real practical requirement, while a functional outfit need not become a fashion project. User-stated intentions outrank an inferred persona. Do not claim to diagnose authenticity, character, or personal history from a photograph. A clearer or neater alternative can still be the wrong improvement if it removes the quality the user values. [F2, F5, C1]

Keep construction quality, ensemble compatibility, actual usefulness, and pleasure in wearing distinct. Technically impressive individual products do not necessarily assemble into a satisfying outfit. Nor does a neutral or expensive item automatically earn a central role in a wardrobe: a patterned or expressive item can be foundational if it repeatedly supports the user's real combinations. A simple capsule is also valid for someone prioritizing convenience. [F1, C4, C5, C9]

## Learn from examples without buying the whole look

When a user likes an inspiration image, first identify the particular attraction: a generous coat over narrow trousers, an expansive collar, repeated pattern, the color-area relationship, or the overall mood. Different attractions require different substitutions. Do not reduce the image to a brand list or assume every garment category must be copied. [F5, F8, C4]

Translate one relationship using known existing clothes. Ask what they own only when needed; never invent their wardrobe. Try the combination, compare the actual outline or wear experience, and keep a small record of what worked if the user wants it. Distinguish inspirational images from outfits tested on the wearer. A flat lay cannot establish the drape or scale of the worn result. Revisiting favorites before shopping can reveal what was genuinely appealing and what was just novelty. No elaborate catalogue or new app is required. [F5, C8]

For uncertainty, offer a controlled comparison rather than a replacement shopping list. Preserve the original idea, vary one consequential element, and explain what the alternative would test. If the goal itself is open, show a path toward restraint and one toward stronger expression, without silently making the quieter option the correct answer. [F6, C1, C8]

## Longevity includes wanting to wear it

Distinguish physical durability from continued attachment. An item may remain intact yet go unworn; a worn item may remain a favorite. Consider expected aging, care, actual outfit use, and the user's enjoyment rather than relying on a timeless label, versatility claim, or premium construction. Patina and irregularity are not always damage, nor always improvements. Do not demand that denim behave like wool trousers. [F2, F4, F7]

Do not make emotional value, maintenance rituals, or style exploration obligations. Preferences can change without guilt. Recommend replacement or acquisition only when its role in wanted outfits is clear; technical superiority alone is not enough. Never promise that clothes will transform a life. [F4, C5]
